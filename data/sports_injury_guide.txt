SPORTS INJURY FIRST AID AND RECOVERY GUIDE
==========================================
A Reference for Athletes and Sports Coaches
Source: General Sports Medicine and Physiotherapy Principles


SECTION 1: THE RICE AND PRICE PROTOCOLS
========================================

The RICE Protocol — Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation
------------------------------------------------------
The RICE protocol is the cornerstone of first-aid treatment for acute soft-tissue
sports injuries such as sprains, strains, and bruises. It should be applied within
the first 24–72 hours of injury to limit swelling, reduce pain, and promote healing.

REST
The injured area must be rested immediately. Continuing activity after injury
increases tissue damage and prolongs recovery time. For lower-limb injuries,
use crutches if needed to keep weight off the area.

ICE
Apply a cold pack, ice wrapped in a damp cloth, or a bag of frozen vegetables
to the injured area for 15–20 minutes. Repeat every 2–3 hours for the first
48–72 hours. Never apply ice directly to bare skin as it can cause ice burns.
Cold therapy reduces inflammation and numbs the area to decrease pain.

COMPRESSION
Wrap the injured area firmly with a compression bandage (e.g. a crepe bandage)
to reduce swelling. Start from below the injury and work upward. Ensure the
bandage is not so tight that it cuts off circulation — check that fingers or toes
remain warm and pink. Loosen if there is increased pain, numbness, or tingling.

ELEVATION
Raise the injured limb above the level of the heart whenever possible, especially
in the first 24 hours. This uses gravity to drain excess fluid away from the area,
reducing swelling significantly.

The PRICE Protocol — Protection, Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation
-------------------------------------------------------------------
PRICE adds Protection to the RICE protocol:

PROTECTION
In the immediate period following injury, protect the area from further harm.
This may mean removing a boot or glove carefully, stopping all play immediately,
using a splint or sling where appropriate, or having the athlete carried off
the field rather than walking.

When to Use PRICE vs RICE
PRICE is preferred for more severe injuries or where there is risk of further
damage. RICE is appropriate for minor sprains and muscle strains. If in doubt,
always apply protection first.


SECTION 2: ANKLE SPRAINS
=========================

Overview
Ankle sprains are the most common sports injury, particularly in football,
basketball, netball, and running. They occur when the ankle is rolled, twisted,
or turned beyond its normal range of motion, damaging the ligaments.

Grades of Ankle Sprain
- Grade 1 (Mild): Minor stretching and microscopic tearing of ligament fibres.
  Mild tenderness, swelling. Can bear weight. Recovery: 1–3 weeks.
- Grade 2 (Moderate): Partial tearing of the ligament. Moderate pain, swelling,
  bruising, and instability. Difficult to bear weight. Recovery: 3–6 weeks.
- Grade 3 (Severe): Complete ligament rupture. Severe swelling, bruising,
  significant instability. Cannot bear weight. May need imaging to rule out fracture.
  Recovery: 6–12 weeks, sometimes longer.

Immediate Treatment
Apply the PRICE protocol immediately. Do not attempt to "walk it off."
Seek medical evaluation if you suspect a Grade 3 sprain or fracture.
The Ottawa Ankle Rules indicate imaging is required if there is bone tenderness
along the back edge of the lower fibula, the back edge of the tibia,
or inability to bear weight for four steps.

Rehabilitation Phases
Phase 1 (Days 1–3): PRICE. No weight bearing. Pain management.
Phase 2 (Days 3–14): Gentle range of motion exercises. Partial weight bearing
with support. Pool walking if available.
Phase 3 (Week 2–6): Strengthening exercises. Single-leg balance. Proprioception
training. Progressive return to weight bearing.
Phase 4 (Week 4–12): Sports-specific drills. Return to training. Ankle bracing
for the first few weeks back.

Red Flags — See a Doctor Immediately
- Inability to bear weight at all
- Significant deformity of the joint
- Numbness or pins and needles below the ankle
- Severe bruising within the first hour
- Suspected fracture


SECTION 3: KNEE INJURIES
=========================

Overview
Knee injuries are common in sports involving pivoting, jumping, and contact.
The most frequently injured structures are the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL),
the medial collateral ligament (MCL), the menisci (cartilage pads), and the
patellar tendon.

Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) Injury
The ACL is responsible for rotational stability of the knee. ACL injuries often
occur with sudden deceleration, pivoting, or direct contact. A characteristic
"pop" is often heard or felt at the time of injury, followed by rapid swelling
(within 2 hours), instability, and inability to continue playing.

ACL injuries require specialist assessment (MRI scan). Partial tears may be
managed conservatively with physiotherapy; complete ruptures usually require
surgical reconstruction followed by 9–12 months of rehabilitation.

Do NOT attempt to return to sport without specialist clearance after an ACL injury.

Medial Collateral Ligament (MCL) Injury
The MCL is on the inner side of the knee and is commonly injured by a direct
blow to the outer knee or excessive valgus force (knee caving inward).
Grade 1–2 MCL tears are usually treated conservatively: RICE, bracing, and
progressive physiotherapy. Grade 3 tears may need surgical review.
Recovery: 2–8 weeks depending on severity.

Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome (Runner's Knee)
Aching pain around or behind the kneecap, worsened by running, stairs, squatting,
or sitting for long periods. Caused by poor patellar tracking, weak quadriceps or
hip muscles, and overtraining. Treatment: activity modification, ice, quadriceps
and hip strengthening, correct running technique. Recovery: 4–12 weeks.

Meniscal Tears
The menisci are C-shaped cartilage pads that act as shock absorbers. A torn
meniscus typically presents with localised joint-line tenderness, swelling,
clicking, catching or locking sensations, and difficulty fully bending or
straightening the knee. MRI is required for diagnosis. Treatment depends on the
type and location of the tear — many are managed conservatively; others require
arthroscopic surgery.

Immediate Treatment for Knee Injuries
Apply PRICE. Do not twist or force the knee. Seek medical assessment if the knee
is locked, unable to fully extend, severely swollen, or very unstable.


SECTION 4: SHOULDER INJURIES IN SPORT
=======================================

Overview
Shoulder injuries are common in throwing sports (cricket, baseball, javelin),
racket sports (tennis, badminton), swimming, and contact sports.
The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, which makes it inherently
less stable and more vulnerable to injury.

Rotator Cuff Strains and Tears
The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus,
teres minor, subscapularis) that stabilise the shoulder. Overuse, repetitive
overhead movements, or acute trauma can strain or tear these muscles.

Symptoms: Pain on shoulder movement (especially raising the arm above 90°),
weakness, and aching at night.

Treatment: RICE for acute injury, rest from overhead activities, physiotherapy
focused on rotator cuff strengthening and scapular stability. Partial tears
respond well to physiotherapy; complete tears may require surgical repair.
Recovery: 6 weeks to 6 months depending on severity.

Shoulder Dislocation
Dislocation occurs when the head of the humerus pops out of the shoulder socket,
usually anteriorly (forward). It is common in contact sports, rugby, and football.

Signs: Intense pain, obvious deformity, inability to move the arm, the arm
held away from the body.

CRITICAL: Do NOT attempt to put the shoulder back yourself. This requires trained
medical personnel and often requires muscle relaxants. Immobilise the arm
with a sling, apply ice, and seek immediate emergency medical attention.

After formal reduction by a doctor, rehabilitation includes progressive range of
motion, rotator cuff strengthening, and sport-specific exercises.
Recovery: 3–6 months; risk of recurrence is high in young athletes.

Shoulder Impingement Syndrome
Repetitive overhead movements can cause tendons to be pinched under the acromion
bone. Symptoms include a dull ache at the top/front of the shoulder, pain when
reaching overhead or behind the back, and painful arc of movement between 60–120°.
Treatment: Activity modification, ice, NSAIDs (on medical advice), physiotherapy
with posterior capsule stretching and strengthening.


SECTION 5: MUSCLE STRAINS
==========================

Overview
A muscle strain (or "pulled muscle") occurs when muscle fibres are overstretched
or torn. They are classified by grade:

- Grade 1 (Mild): Minor fibre tearing. Tightness and localised tenderness.
  No significant loss of strength. Recovery: 1–2 weeks.
- Grade 2 (Moderate): Partial muscle tear. Significant pain, swelling, bruising.
  Reduced strength. Recovery: 3–6 weeks.
- Grade 3 (Severe): Complete muscle rupture. Severe pain, significant disability,
  possible palpable defect in the muscle. Often requires surgical consultation.
  Recovery: 3–6 months.

Hamstring Strains
Hamstrings (muscles at the back of the thigh) are the most commonly strained
muscles in sprinting sports — athletics, football, rugby, and cricket.
They often occur during explosive acceleration or high-speed running.

Immediate treatment: Stop activity immediately. Apply PRICE. Do not stretch
the hamstring in the acute phase. Seek physiotherapy assessment.

Return to sport should be gradual and guided by a physiotherapist.
Premature return to sport is the main cause of re-injury, which can result in
a chronic problem. A full hamstring strain rehabilitation programme typically
lasts 6–12 weeks.

Calf Strains
Calf strains (gastrocnemius or soleus) are common in running and racket sports.
Often described as a sudden sharp pain in the back of the lower leg, as if kicked.
Apply PRICE immediately. Use heel raises inside shoes to reduce calf tension.
Avoid stretching in the acute phase. Recovery: 1–6 weeks depending on grade.

Quadriceps Strains
The quadriceps at the front of the thigh can be strained during explosive
kicking or sprinting. Apply PRICE. Gentle knee flexion exercises begin after 48h.
Recovery: 2–6 weeks.

Muscle Cramps
Cramps are sudden, involuntary, and painful contractions of a muscle. They are
common during or after exercise, especially in hot conditions with dehydration
or electrolyte imbalance.

Immediate treatment: Gently stretch and massage the affected muscle. Rehydrate
with water or a sports drink containing electrolytes. Do not resume intense
activity until the cramp has fully resolved and the muscle feels relaxed.
Prevention: adequate hydration before and during sport, warm-up and cool-down,
balanced diet with potassium and magnesium.


SECTION 6: HEAD INJURIES AND CONCUSSION
=========================================

Overview
Concussion is a traumatic brain injury caused by a direct or indirect blow to
the head, face, or neck. It is serious and must never be ignored.

Signs and Symptoms of Concussion
- Headache or pressure in the head
- Nausea or vomiting
- Dizziness or balance problems
- Blurred or double vision
- Sensitivity to light or noise
- Feeling "foggy" or confused
- Memory problems (can't remember events before or after the incident)
- Loss of consciousness (may be brief)
- Slurred speech
- Abnormal behaviour or personality change

CRITICAL RULE — "If in doubt, sit them out"
Any athlete suspected of having a concussion must be removed from play immediately
and must NOT return to play on the same day, regardless of how well they feel.
The athlete must be assessed by a medical professional before returning to sport.

Concussion Management — Return to Sport Protocol
Once all symptoms have fully resolved at rest, the athlete must follow a
graduated return-to-sport programme:
1. Light aerobic exercise (walking, swimming)
2. Sport-specific exercise (running drills)
3. Non-contact training drills
4. Full-contact practice (after medical clearance)
5. Return to competition

Each step takes a minimum of 24 hours. If symptoms return at any step, go back
to the previous step after being symptom-free for 24 hours.

EMERGENCY — Seek immediate medical attention if:
- Loss of consciousness lasting more than 1–2 minutes
- Seizures
- Repeated vomiting
- Worsening headache
- Unequal pupils
- Clear fluid from the nose or ears
- Weakness or numbness in limbs


SECTION 7: FRACTURES AND DISLOCATIONS
========================================

Overview
Fractures (broken bones) and dislocations (joint out of place) are serious
injuries that require immediate medical attention. Sports carry risk of fractures
from falls, collisions, or impact with equipment.

Signs of a Fracture
- Severe pain at the injury site, worsened by movement or pressure
- Obvious deformity or angulation
- Rapid, significant swelling
- Bruising over the injury site
- A "snap" or "crack" heard at the time of injury
- Inability to use the injured limb
- Numbness or tingling (suggests nerve involvement)
- In open fractures: bone visible through the skin — EMERGENCY

First Aid for Suspected Fractures
- Stop the athlete immediately
- Do NOT try to straighten a deformed limb
- Immobilise the injury: support the limb in the position found using a splint
  (a rolled newspaper or magazine can work in an emergency) or a sling for arm injuries
- Apply ice wrapped in cloth to reduce swelling
- Seek immediate medical attention (emergency department)
- Treat for shock if necessary: keep the athlete warm, lie them down, and keep
  them calm
- For open fractures: cover the wound with a clean cloth; do NOT attempt to
  push the bone back in. Call emergency services immediately.

Dislocations
A dislocation is when the bones of a joint are forced out of their normal position.
Common in the shoulder, finger, and kneecap.
- Do NOT attempt to relocate the joint yourself
- Immobilise in the current position
- Apply ice and seek immediate medical or emergency care


SECTION 8: KARATE AND MARTIAL ARTS SPECIFIC INJURIES
======================================================

Common Injuries in Karate
Karate and similar martial arts carry a specific injury profile due to striking,
kicking, blocking, and falling techniques.

Hand and Wrist Injuries
- Boxer's fracture: fracture of the 4th or 5th metacarpal from improper punching
  technique. Signs: knuckle area pain and swelling, depressed knuckle. Requires
  X-ray and medical management.
- Wrist sprains: common from falls or blocking. Apply PRICE. If severe or
  persistent, an X-ray is needed to rule out scaphoid fracture (often missed —
  persistent snuffbox tenderness warrants imaging even if initial X-ray is normal).

Foot and Toe Injuries
- Toe sprains and dislocations from kicking with incorrect technique
- Metatarsal stress fractures from repetitive footwork on hard training floors
- Apply PRICE; seek medical review for suspected fractures

Rib Injuries
- Rib contusions from blocking are common in kumite (sparring)
- Symptoms: localised rib pain, worse on deep breathing or movement
- Rest from contact training. Rib fractures require hospital assessment.

Head and Face Injuries
- All head injuries in martial arts must be taken seriously
- Refer to Section 6 for concussion assessment and management
- Facial lacerations and broken noses require medical assessment

Injury Prevention in Karate
- Always train with properly fitted protective equipment (gloves, shin guards,
  mouthguard, groin guard where applicable)
- Ensure correct technique before increasing intensity
- Warm up thoroughly before training
- Tap out early in grappling — do not push through joint pain
- Train on appropriate matted surfaces
- Ensure medical cover is present at competitions


SECTION 9: OVERUSE INJURIES AND TENDINOPATHY
=============================================

Overview
Overuse injuries occur gradually from repetitive stress rather than a single
traumatic event. They are common in endurance sports and sports with repetitive
technique demands.

Tendinopathy (Tendon Pain)
Formerly called tendinitis, tendinopathy is now recognised as a degeneration
of the tendon structure rather than simple inflammation.

Common sites: Achilles tendon, patellar tendon (jumper's knee), rotator cuff
tendons, and the lateral elbow tendons (tennis elbow).

Symptoms: Gradual onset of localised pain and stiffness, worse in the morning
and at the start of exercise (warms up then returns after cooling down).

Treatment:
1. Load management — reduce but do not completely rest. Total rest is counterproductive.
2. Eccentric loading exercises — the strongest evidence-based treatment
   (e.g. Alfredson heel-drop protocol for Achilles tendinopathy)
3. Ice after activity for pain management
4. Avoid aggravating activities in the short term
5. Physiotherapy guidance is essential

Recovery: 6–24 weeks depending on severity and compliance with rehab.

Shin Splints (Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome)
A common overuse injury in runners and jumping athletes. Pain along the inner
edge of the shinbone, often in both legs. Caused by repetitive impact on
hard surfaces, sudden increases in training load, or biomechanical issues.

Treatment: Reduce training load. Replace hard-surface training with swimming
or cycling. Ice after activity. Review running footwear. Gradual return to
running over 4–8 weeks.

Stress Fractures
Repeated loading can cause microscopic cracks in bone that progress to a stress
fracture. Common in the metatarsals, tibia, and fibula in runners.
Signs: Focal, pinpoint bone tenderness, pain that starts during activity and
persists after. Often normal on initial X-ray — MRI or bone scan may be needed.
Treatment: Non-weight-bearing rest, boot or crutches in severe cases.
MUST be assessed by a medical professional. Return to sport takes 6–16 weeks.


SECTION 10: GENERAL RECOVERY PRINCIPLES
=========================================

Hydration and Nutrition
Adequate hydration is critical for both performance and recovery. Dehydration
impairs muscle function and increases injury risk. Drink water consistently
throughout the day and during training. Use sports drinks for sessions exceeding
60–90 minutes to replace electrolytes.

Protein intake supports muscle repair. Aim for 1.6–2.2g of protein per kg of
body weight per day when recovering from a muscle injury.

Sleep and Rest
Sleep is the body's primary recovery mechanism. During deep sleep, growth hormone
is released, which stimulates tissue repair. Athletes should aim for 7–9 hours
per night. Sleep deprivation increases injury risk and slows recovery.

Progressive Loading
When returning from injury, do not rush. Gradual progressive loading allows
the healing tissue to adapt. The general rule is: increase load by no more than
10% per week. Pain should be used as a guide — discomfort up to a 3/10 is
generally acceptable during rehab; sharp or worsening pain means stop.

Psychological Recovery
Injury can have a significant psychological impact, including frustration,
anxiety, and fear of re-injury. Acknowledge these feelings. Set short-term
goals during rehabilitation to maintain motivation. Seek support from
sports psychologists if fear of re-injury is preventing return to sport.

When to See a Physiotherapist
Seek physiotherapy if:
- Pain is not improving within 5–7 days of self-care
- You are unsure about the severity of an injury
- You are returning from a significant injury and need a structured programme
- You have recurring injuries in the same area

When to Seek Emergency Medical Care
- Suspected fracture or dislocation
- Head injury or suspected concussion
- Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or loss of consciousness
- Severe or uncontrolled bleeding
- Suspected spinal injury — do not move the athlete
- Any injury where you are unsure of the severity
